Catcher Framing, 2026ß

Abstract:
The strike zone is not a rectangle — and the numbers can't tell catchers apart.
Standfirst (one text object directly under the title)
Every taken pitch of the 2026 MLB regular season: 356,498 pitches across 184 game days, from Statcast. Where umpires actually call strikes, which catchers appear to earn the most, and why — once you control for where their pitchers were throwing — almost none of that difference survives.
That's the 30-second read. A viewer who stops there should leave with the argument intact.

View 1 — The empirical strike zone
Title
The called zone is a baseball wider than the plate
Caption
Called-strike rate on taken pitches, by location. The box marks the rulebook zone: home plate's 17 inches wide, and the league-average vertical bounds (1.62–3.21 ft) measured from this data. The dashed lines sit a baseball's radius outside the plate edge.
Strikes are called well past the plate — and the boundary lands almost exactly on the dashed lines. That isn't umpires being generous. A pitch is a strike if any part of the ball crosses the zone, so the true boundary is the plate edge plus 1.45 inches. The naive rectangle was the wrong rectangle.
The band where the call is a coin flip is narrower than the "fuzzy edge" framing suggests. Everything a catcher can influence happens inside it.

View 2 — Raw rates by catcher
Title
Called strike rate on takes, by catcher
Caption
Catchers with at least 3,000 taken pitches in 2026, sorted by the share of called strikes. Dot size shows sample. The line is league average, 32.4%.
The spread runs 34.2% to 30.4% — 3.8 percentage points, or roughly 200 extra called strikes across a season.
This is not a framing leaderboard. A catcher receiving pitchers with better command collects more strikes without doing anything. The next panels test that — and then test whether anything survives it.
Naming the confound here, before the viewer forms a conclusion, is the point. A reader who takes this chart at face value and is corrected two panels later has already been misled once.

View 3 — The zone by count
Title
The zone changes shape with the count
Caption
Called-strike rate by location, split by who's ahead. Hitter's counts (1-0, 2-0, 3-0, 2-1, 3-1) hold 82,536 takes; even counts (0-0, 1-1, 3-2) hold 161,855; pitcher's counts (0-1, 0-2, 1-2, 2-2) hold 112,107. 3-2 counts as even by convention rather than by a mechanical balls-vs-strikes rule.
The zone expands when the hitter is ahead because the hitter is more willing to take a close pitch. When the hitter is ahead, they are hunting a pitch in the middle of the zone.
Specific counts were abandoned for grouped ones: 3-0 holds only 6,999 takes, about 17 per location bin, which is too thin to read.

View 4 — Framing, location-controlled
Title
Called strikes above expected, by location
Caption
For each location bin, the league called-strike rate in that bin is the expected probability — a pitch down the middle carries an expectation near 0.98, one six inches outside near 0.05. Red means a catcher got more strikes than the location deserved; blue, fewer. Pitcher command largely cancels out, because meeting expectation earns no credit.
Individual cells are within sampling noise. At roughly 50 pitches per cell for one catcher-season, the standard error on a cell is about 7 percentage points. Read the broad pattern, not single squares — and note that framing can only operate at the edges, where the call was in doubt. Color in the middle of the zone is noise.

View 5 — Staff vs skill
Title
The hand they were dealt vs what they did with it
Caption
Horizontal: expected called-strike rate, effectively an index of how well that catcher's pitchers located. Vertical: called strikes above expected across the season. Each dot is a qualified catcher.
The relationship is negative and significant — slope −1,328, p = 0.011, R² = 0.11. Catchers with better-locating staffs post lower strikes above expected, by about 56 strikes across the observed range.
That is the opposite of what an umpire "rhythm" effect would predict, and it means location control is incomplete. Three explanations fit and this data cannot separate them: sequential effects in umpire calls (published work finds umpires are less likely to call a strike right after calling one); within-bin pitch placement (good-command pitchers aim at the edge, so their pitches inside an edge bin sit toward the outside, below the bin's average); and roster construction (teams with elite pitching can afford a bat-first catcher).

The precision panel — the most important text on the dashboard
Place this immediately after the three-catcher table. It is the conclusion the rest of the dashboard earns.
Title
The measurement isn't precise enough to rank catchers
Body
Across 54 qualified catchers, the observed spread in strikes above expected is 33.5. The spread you would expect from chance alone, given how many pitches each catcher received, is 33.9.
They are the same number. Subtracting one variance from the other leaves nothing — the estimated true difference between catchers is zero. Every apparent gap in the leaderboard above, from +69 to −64, is consistent with 54 draws from a distribution centred on zero.
This is a statement about the method, not about framing. Published metrics do find consistent, repeatable differences between catchers across seasons. The approach here — 0.25-ft location bins, with no adjustment for count, handedness, pitch type or pitcher — is too coarse to resolve an effect that operates at the scale of inches.
The residual slope against staff quality on the panel above points the same way: this metric is partly measuring where pitchers throw within a bin, not how catchers receive. Two independent lines of evidence, one conclusion.
Both findings specify what the next version needs: a smooth model over location with pitcher effects, replacing bins that average away the exact margin framing works in.
On tone. Resist softening this into "results were inconclusive." The point is not that the analysis failed — it is that the analysis produced a number and then produced the arithmetic showing that number cannot support the conclusion a reader would otherwise draw from it. That is the finding.

About this project (Tableau Public description)
Catcher framing, 2026 MLB regular season
Every taken pitch of the season — 356,498 across 184 game days — pulled from Statcast and run through a pipeline I built end to end: a Python ingest with round-trip verification, Parquet landed in Google Cloud Storage, partitioned loads into BigQuery with atomic re-run semantics, dbt models with 27 tests, and a scheduled daily refresh.
The question: can you see catcher framing in public data, and can you separate it from the quality of the pitching staff?
The strike zone itself is visibly not the rulebook rectangle — it's a baseball's radius wider, which turns out to be the rule working correctly rather than umpires erring. Raw called-strike rate ranks catchers in an order that mostly reflects their pitchers: the league leader catches the staff with the best locations in baseball, the last-place catcher the worst, and both move to average once location is controlled.
And then the answer to the second question is no — at least not this way. The spread between catchers after controlling for location is 33.5 strikes; the spread expected from chance alone is 33.9. The method can't tell them apart, and the dashboard shows the arithmetic rather than the ranking.

Method and limitations (a text object, or the second half of the description)
How expected strikes are computed. Every taken pitch is assigned to a 0.25-ft location bin. The league called-strike rate within that bin is the expected probability. A catcher's "strikes above expected" is the sum of actual strikes minus the sum of expectations across all their takes. The baseline is computed in the warehouse as a tested dbt model, not in Tableau.
Vertical location is normalized per batter. The strike zone's top and bottom are set by each hitter's stance, so a pitch at 2.5 feet is a strike to one batter and a ball to another. Heights are expressed relative to each batter's own zone, then rescaled onto the league-average zone (1.62–3.21 ft) so the axis reads in feet.
What's included. Regular season only. Taken pitches only — balls and called strikes. Excluded: swings, bunts, hit-by-pitch, pitchouts, blocked balls (the pitch bounced; the catcher was blocking, not framing), automatic ball and strike rulings (no pitch was thrown), and 192 pitches with no recorded location.
Three limitations, in order of size.
The method cannot distinguish catchers at one season. Observed spread 33.5 strikes, chance-alone spread 33.9. See the precision panel.
Binning attenuates. A 0.25-ft bin at the zone edge contains pitches whose true strike probability ranges from about 0.2 to 0.8. Averaging across that gradient discards exactly the margin framing operates in — and introduces a measurable bias, visible as the negative slope against staff quality.
No other covariates. Count, handedness and pitch type all shift the called zone and aren't conditioned on here.
All three point the same direction: replace bins with a model. Logistic regression or a GAM over location, handedness, count and pitcher pools information across neighbouring locations instead of treating each cell as an independent sample, and a pitcher term would absorb the within-bin placement effect directly. That's the next phase.
